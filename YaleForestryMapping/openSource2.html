About This Map

This is an interactive historical map tracing the life paths of Yale Forest School alumni, cohorts of 1902 through 1912. The purpose of the map is to show the influence of the Yale Forest School in the first twelve years of its existence. The map demonstrates the wide range of places that Yale Forest School alumni worked, and the types of activities that they were engaged in. By comparing an alumnus' pre-Yale positions and activities to their post-Yale positions and activities the viewer can get a sense of each alumnus' “knowledge of place” or lack thereof.1

Each alumnus is plotted as a sequence of points connecting the places that shaped their individual careers. Plotted here are birth locations, positions and activities before pursuing a M.F. at the Yale Forest School, and their different positions and activities after leaving Yale or graduating. This map is not a complete life history of all alumni, nor does this map perfectly represent the chronological life trajectories of all alumni. It is important to note that not all alumni pursued forestry in a professional capacity after their time at Yale.

Use the Cohort Navigation panel to filter the view by cohort year, by individual alumnus, or by life-stage (pre-Yale versus post-Yale), and hover or click any marker to read the underlying archival record.

Location Markers (the dots)

  • Alumnus Birthplace — recorded native origin of the alumnus.
  • Pre-Yale Positions/Activities — a position or activity before enrolling at Yale. Each institution or position is its own point.
  • Yale Forest School (Graduated) — the Yale Forest School in New Haven, shown for those who earned the M.F. degree or a certificate.
  • Yale Forest School (Non-Graduated) — New Haven, shown as a square for students who attended but did not complete a degree.
  • Post-Yale Positions/Activities — each position or activity after leaving Yale, plotted by city and state.

Trajectory Lines

  • Birthplace Origin Path — links a birthplace to the first subsequent position or activity location.
  • Pre-Yale Paths — connects successive pre-Yale positions or activities in chronological order.
  • Move to New Haven — the step from the last pre-Yale position or activity location to the Yale Forest School.
  • Post-Yale Paths — trace the ordered sequence of position or activity locations after Yale.

Cohorts & Categories

The cohort navigation separates each graduating year into three cohorts: M.F. (Master of Forestry degree holders), Certificate (attended and certified but not degreed), and Non-Graduate (attended without completing). Each is selectable individually in the Cohort Year filter, so a single class year may be examined as up to three distinct populations.

Average Career Distance from Yale

When the “Avg. Career Distance from Yale” option is enabled and a single cohort is selected, a dashed red circle is drawn centered on Yale (New Haven). The radius represents that cohort’s mean distance from Yale to its members’ post-Yale positions or activities. The figure is person-weighted: each alumnus’s own average position-or-activity distance is computed first, and the cohort figure is the average of those individual means. Every alumnus counts equally regardless of how many postings they held. Remember, not all alumni worked in professional forestry. The accompanying panel reports the distance in miles and kilometers, the number of alumni and postings involved, and a bar scaling the cohort mean against its farthest individual average.

Source of records: Biographical data are drawn from Biographical Record of the Graduates and Former Students of the Yale Forest School. Geographic coordinates were derived from the locations named in each biography; where a source named only a state, region, or country, the relevant capital of that location was used.

1 The concept of knowledge of place has deep intellectual roots but the authors first became familiar with the term through the work of William Cronon, specifically William Cronon, “Landscape and Home: Environmental Traditions in Wisconsin,” The Wisconsin Magazine of History 74, no. 2 (1990): 82–105. James C. Scott uses another term, “local knowledge,” to the same effect. For Scott, local knowledge is distinct from his core concept of metis but the two ideas support one another in many instances. See James C. Scott, Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed (Yale University Press, 2020).

2 A note on the average-distance ring and its accuracy. The distance figures reported in the panel (miles and kilometers) are computed as true great-circle distances on the globe and are accurate. The red ring, however, is drawn as a screen circle — a shape that is perfectly round on your display — rather than as a true geographic circle. This is a deliberate legibility choice. This map, like most web maps, uses the Web Mercator projection, which progressively stretches the north–south scale as one moves away from the equator. On such a projection a true circle of equal ground distance in every direction (a “true radial” or isodistance ring) does not appear round at all; centered on New Haven it renders as a vertically elongated egg, and the distortion grows with the radius. To avoid presenting viewers with a lopsided shape that reads as an error, the ring here is instead sized in screen pixels from the cohort’s mean distance measured along the east–west axis at Yale’s latitude. The consequence is that the ring is precise along that east–west axis but slightly overstates ground distance toward the north and south: a place touching the ring due north of Yale is somewhat closer, in real miles, than a place touching it due east. The ring should therefore be read as a schematic indicator of a cohort’s average spread from Yale, not as a strict boundary within which every member fell. For exact distances, consult the numeric readout in the panel, which is unaffected by this projection tradeoff.
